## B1

### [CONTEXT] B1 | Ordering arepas and tinto at a neighbourhood stall

This context page prepares learners for the Gen 3 culture lesson **Ordering arepas and tinto at a neighbourhood stall** in Colombian Spanish. The lesson is about a realistic cultural situation where learners need language they can actually say, not trivia to memorise. In practice, the speaking activity helps them build simple phrases for ordering Colombian street snacks, coffee, prices, and friendly small talk.

Colombia is strongly regional, so the same interaction can feel different in Bogotá, Medellín, Cali, the Caribbean coast, the coffee region, or a smaller town. Use this page to frame the lesson as a real-life Colombian Spanish situation, not just a list of phrases. Food situations are especially useful because they naturally include greetings, prices, preferences, recommendations, politeness, quick reactions, and small negotiations.

Before starting the lesson, tell students to focus on three things: the situation, the relationship between the speakers, and the tone. They should notice which phrases are safe for everyday use, which ones are informal, and which ones depend on trust or context. The goal is to leave the lesson able to handle this situation naturally in conversation.

### [CONTEXT] B1 | Ordering menú at a Peruvian lunch spot

This context page prepares learners for the Gen 3 culture lesson **Ordering menú at a Peruvian lunch spot** in Peruvian Spanish. The lesson is about a realistic cultural situation where learners need language they can actually say, not trivia to memorise. In practice, the speaking activity helps them ask for menu options, prices, drinks, and simple food preferences.

Peru has strong regional, class, food, transport, and social-context differences, so the same phrase can feel casual, polite, warm, or risky depending on the scene. Use this page to frame the lesson as a real-life Peruvian Spanish situation, not just a list of phrases.

Before starting the lesson, tell students to focus on three things: the situation, the relationship between the speakers, and the tone. They should notice which phrases are safe for everyday use, which ones are informal, and which ones depend on trust or context. The goal is to leave the lesson able to handle this situation naturally in conversation.

In practice, the speaking activity helps them handle desire, ambiguity, consent, friends watching, and graceful retreat.

Puerto Rican Spanish is fast, expressive, Caribbean, and often shaped by music, community, code-switching, and social closeness. Use this page to frame the lesson as a real-life Puerto Rican Spanish situation, not just a list of phrases. Transport and movement situations are culturally rich because people have to explain delays, routes, crowds, stress, timing, and changes of plan without sounding too stiff. Identity and social nuance topics help learners move beyond vocabulary and notice subtext, register, humour, social risk, and what people avoid saying directly.

Before starting the lesson, tell students to focus on three things: the situation, the relationship between the speakers, and the tone.
